For Journalists

I am glad to speak with reporters, producers, and editors about electricity: what it costs, who pays for it, why the grid is straining under new demand from data centers, and what policy can and cannot fix. I can comment on deadline, on background, or on camera, and I am happy to explain technical material in plain language or point you to a colleague who knows a topic better than I do.

Please email me and copy our communications lead so we can find time quickly:

Yury Dvorkin — ydvorki1@jhu.edu
Hanna Robbins, Johns Hopkins, Media Relationships — hrobbin8@jh.edu
Jill Rosen, Johns Hopkins, Media Relationships — jrosen@jhu.edu

If you are on a same-day deadline, please put your outlet, deadline, and the question you need answered in the subject line. I am based in Baltimore and can join remote interviews at short notice.

I am an associate professor at Johns Hopkins University, appointed in Electrical & Computer Engineering and in Civil & Systems Engineering and affiliated with the Ralph O'Connor Sustainable Energy Institute. I am the US director of the NSF Global Climate Center EPICS and a co-investigator in the DOE offshore wind center ARROW. I have testified before the New York City Council and prepared analyses for the Maryland Department of the Environment, and I serve on the National Academies' roundtable on AI and climate change.

Topics I Can Speak To

  • Data centers, AI, and what electricity costs. How much load data centers actually add and what their flexibility is worth on a given grid; interconnection and siting fights, and why the answer differs between PJM, ERCOT, and elsewhere; how the costs of new large loads get allocated among ratepayers, including in communities like Baltimore; and how US and Chinese generation capacity shapes the technology race.
  • Building the grid: equipment supply chains and transmission. Transformers, switchgear, and UPS systems, import dependence and recent federal actions on it, and why equipment shortages may bind before generation or transmission does; multi-criteria transmission expansion, dynamic line ratings and other grid-enhancing technologies, PJM and Maryland proceedings, and offshore interregional networks.
  • Electricity markets, storage, and resource adequacy. Storage arbitrage and what operators can actually guarantee, capacity accreditation, capacity market reform, market design under extreme events, hurricane-driven offshore wind outage risk in PJM reliability studies, and where machine learning genuinely helps operations and planning versus where its guarantees run out.
  • Reliability, extreme weather, and cybersecurity. Heat waves, storms, and outages; who is most exposed when the power goes out and the security margins systems are planned around; attacks through EV charging and other grid-edge devices, cyber insurance, and lessons from incidents such as the Colonial Pipeline hack.
  • Clean energy claims and electrification. Hourly versus annual matching, the granularity of emissions accounting, transmission limits on matching policies, and how to read corporate clean energy commitments; electrifying chemical manufacturing, hydrogen and electrolysis, nuclear flexibility, industrial demand response, and where EV charging infrastructure gets built and who is left out.

If your story sits next to one of these rather than squarely on it, feel free to write. Time provided, I would rather help you find the right expert than have you guess.
